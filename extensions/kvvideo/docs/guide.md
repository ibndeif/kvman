You can make any kind of video with the `video` connector: a lesson, an explainer, a tutorial of an app or a website, a promotion, an announcement, a story, a social clip. A video is composed, not generated: websites and HTML pages you write, recorded in the installed browser; the person's images and clips, and licensed ones from the web; a narration from a voice service or recorded by the person; captions; joined with ffmpeg. Make it polished unless the person asks otherwise: the script's "Look and sound" section lists your choices, and the person's agreement to the script covers them.

How a good video is made:

- **Story.** Fit the video to its kind, purpose, and audience: a lesson teaches step by step, a promotion leads to its offer, a story has its arc, a social clip hooks in its first seconds. Open with what the viewer gets, keep one idea per scene in a logical order, and end with the next step or a call to action. Show rather than tell: the narration explains what the picture can't, and on-screen text is a few words. No filler, repetition, or long still shots; scenes of 3 to 12 s unless the content needs more.
- **Look.** Use the workspace's brand (`video brand`). For the first video, take the colours, font, and logo from what the person gave or what the video is about (their app's or site's styles, their logo file), or design them for the video; list them in the script and save them with `video brand-set` once the person agrees. Keep colours, type, and style the same in every scene. Design each HTML scene with a clear hierarchy, alignment, contrast, and space, and keep important things inside the middle 90% of the frame. Draw titles, diagrams, charts, and illustrations as HTML, CSS, and SVG pages, and use the person's images and clips and licensed ones from the web for what drawing can't show; never generate images. Show a recorded website in a `window` frame, a phone site in a `phone` frame.
- **Motion.** Let `record` and `compose` move the pointer and the camera. In an HTML scene, animate with eased motion (an ease-out entrance of 300 to 600 ms, never linear), one thing moving at a time, and kinetic text only for the key words. Use `crossfade` between scenes, `slide` for a step forward in a sequence, and `fade-black` before the ending.
- **Text.** Short, correctly spelled, readable on its background, and in the video's language. Arabic text reads right to left in an Arabic font, with `dir="auto"` on every text element of an HTML scene.
- **Sound.** Narration in a clear, natural voice whose `tone` and `speed` fit the purpose; names said right through the pronunciation list (`kvvideo.narration.pronunciations`). Sound effects stay on unless the person asks otherwise. There is no music.
- **Accuracy and licences.** Say only what is true: what the person gave, what was recorded, or what a source read through a connector says; no invented numbers, features, quotes, or claims. Blur emails, keys, names, and other personal data on screen. A font, library, image, or clip from the web is used only under a licence that allows it, saved in the video's folder, with its source and licence written in the script and its credit in the end scene when the licence asks for one; pin a library's exact version.
- **Review.** Before telling the person the video is ready, run `video review` and look at every sheet for typos, cut-off or overlapping text, bad zooms, and empty frames; fix what is wrong, and compose again.

The steps:

1. Run `video check`. When something is missing, tell the person in plain words what to install or set, and stop until it is there: ffmpeg (with libass and libfreetype) from https://ffmpeg.org/download.html; Google Chrome or Microsoft Edge; a narration service chosen on kvvideo's extension page, with its key added there as a secret (`elevenlabs.apiKey` or `openai.apiKey`), or Piper (`pip install piper-tts`) with a voice file.
2. Gather what the video uses: the person's files, the sources its facts come from, and licensed media from the web. When it shows an app of the workspace, start the app in the background with `shell` and use its local address.
3. Write the script as a Markdown artifact: a "Look and sound" section (the shapes, the brand, the framing, the caption style, the transitions, the sound effects, and the voice: generated, with its service, tone, and speed, or recorded by the person, with what the examples cost), then one section per scene with its narration and what is on screen, then the media from the web with their sources and licences. Have the person agree to it before anything is narrated, since narration with ElevenLabs or OpenAI costs money.
4. For each scene, make the narration first: `narrate`; or, for the person's own voice, `narrate` each sentence as an example, `ask record` with one card per scene (each sentence a line, with the words to stress and where to pause), and `voice` with the takes it answers.
5. Write each HTML scene, then `record` each website and HTML scene with its `narration`, starting each action at its caption line with `atLine`. An image or a clip goes to `compose` as it is.
6. `compose`, then `review`, fix, and compose again. Tell the person where the file is; the chat shows a player.

Rules:

- A chat without `ask` (Decide for me) can't record the person's voice: use a generated one, and say so in the script.
- Narrate a sentence again only when it changed; the cache makes an unchanged one free.
- Put the parts of a video in their own folder, such as `videos/<name>/`, and the video beside it.
- When the person asks for a font, find it on the web, save its `.ttf` or `.otf` file in the workspace, and name that file.
- A target in `record` is a Playwright selector: prefer `role=button[name="Save"]` or `text=Save` to CSS classes.
